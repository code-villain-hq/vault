A section opens

Geschichte keeps 47 passages in ten sections, and most are about something that happened at a time. On one page they make a wall to scroll. Two changes make it readable: a section opens on its own, and what happened goes on a timeline. The first is for every topic: Lisa's card in Katzen, with 47 points, opens the same way.

1A section opens on its own

  • On the page, a section shows its first passage, and a card its first four points. The rest is behind "3 more", which opens the section. The page shows what the topic has at a glance; you read one section at a time.
  • Its name opens it too: a section's heading, a card's title, a tile's name. "Almanac › Geschichte › Intifada" leads back.
  • It's everything kept under that name, from every block: its events, its passages, its reminders and open questions, and the talks they came from.
  • Asking from there asks about it. "Ask about Intifada" starts a talk with the section in view, and what it keeps goes into that section.
  • My choice: it opens in the page's place, not in a dialog, so a talk can still open beside it.

2What happened, on a timeline

  • A new kind of block, Timeline: what happened when, oldest first, a line each, with the section it belongs to. Its name opens that section.
  • A point gets a when: a year (1959), a span (1987–1993) or a day (11. September 2001), and "v. Chr." before the year 1. A span's mark is longer than a year's.
  • An event's section is its theme, as for any point: Intifada's five events are the timeline at the top of Intifada.
  • A talk keeps what happened twice: the explanation in its section, and one line with its date on the timeline. For what's kept already, you ask "Mach eine Zeitleiste daraus": a better layout shows it.
  • My choice: a list, not a drawn axis. From 1816 to 2001 an axis would be mostly empty decades, and a list reads on a phone too.
